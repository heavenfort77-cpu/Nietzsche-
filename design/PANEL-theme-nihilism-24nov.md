## Blind round

Written before any seat saw the owner's intent. Question: what does the play alone say about life; score 1-5 how strongly the play makes the player feel life is against him; harshest objection; one change.

Scores (1-5): Story writer 3, Dazai 3, Chaplin 2, Psychologist 3, Core gamer 2, Jam judge 3, Auguste 3, Mobile producer 3, Casual 4, Streamer 4, Nietzsche 3, Camus 3, Critic 3 (median 3). All 13 read the play the same way: hurt yourself on someone else's beat, get paid in a coin you spend to stay standing, skill thickens the mask and arms the double. 12 of 13 said the hostility is told, not felt in the hands on night 1.

### Story writer
1. You get love by hurting yourself to someone else's beat. The Laughs you earn that way don't belong to you: you spend them just to stay standing, they don't carry over, and standing still makes them drain away. Being good at it makes things worse. Every Perfect adds paint and gives more strength to the double who will turn every act you learned against you.
2. 3/5. The systems point the right way: skill feeds the enemy, the Light comes back wider, stillness gnaws. But on night 1 the cost stays in a ledger the player can't feel. A miss is cheap, losing costs only half your shards, and the stranger played the whole night without "feeling hunted".
3. The ending takes back what the mechanics argue. For three nights the play says the mask you build to belong becomes your enemy. Then the player beats that enemy, a warm light of his own falls on him, and "I quit" comes as a prize. Leaving only after a victory says life is against you but you can win and walk free. That is the opposite of Dazai and of Evangelion's last episode, where release comes from letting go, not from a boss kill. The "one last act" button is the more honest of the two endings, yet it reads as the bad one. On top of that, the player never has a way to refuse the act. Complicity that isn't chosen isn't felt.
4. Offer "I quit" during the Performer fight, while the player is under threat, so walking out is a surrender he has to choose rather than a reward for winning.

### Dazai
1. You are paid only for being hurt, and the better you fake the fall, the more paint goes on and the stronger your double grows. The laughter you earn is not kept. You spend it just to stay alive behind the glass, where standing still is itself a crime. Life buys your humiliation, then sends you the bill for having sold it well.
2. 3. The rules punish skill and punish rest, which is right, but the stranger bought upgrades without pain and was let through after a silent house, so the play does not yet hunt him.
3. My fear was never the fall. It was being seen through. When Takeichi whispered "wazato, wazato" (on purpose, on purpose), I wanted the earth to swallow me, and I spent years trying to win him over so the secret would not get out. Your child says the same line, "...on purpose", and it is the best thing in the night, as the stranger saw. But in play it costs nothing. It is one caption, then a corridor. The player never has to hide anything from anyone. He just hits the crash on time and is rewarded. The shame in this game sits in meters and quote cards, my quote cards, while the hands feel only a rhythm game. A clown who is never in danger of being found out is not Yōzō. He is just a clown.
4. Whenever the child is watching, a Perfect fall is the one he sees through, and that seen-through fall, not a miss, is what feeds the Mirror World most.

### Chaplin
1. You fall on someone else's beat, and the better you fall, the more they pay you in a coin you must then burn just to stay on your feet. Skill thickens the paint and feeds the double in the glass. At the end the only way out is to walk out, or to go on performing.
2. 2 of 5. The cruelty is in the arithmetic and the captions, not in the floor. By the stranger's own account, nothing he did made him feel hunted.
3. The fall is the heart of the picture, and here it is a button on a drumroll. A fall is not a timing. It is a body losing its dignity and then fighting to get it back: the stagger, the hat rolling away, the brushing-off, the little bow to cover the shame. That recovery is where the laugh turns to pathos, and the player never gets to do it. He is sixty pixels tall behind a wall of heads, watching a thirty-pixel stick. Worse, nothing in this world is against him. The crew hands him the cable, the crowd pays for the bruise, and he trips himself on purpose every time. Life does not wait for your cue. It puts the banana peel where you are not looking. Here the clown sets every trap himself, so the game is not out to get him. He is out to get himself, and the play turns that into a chore when it should be a wound.
4. Let the world trip him without warning, with no drumroll he chose, and make the player's skill the rescue: turning a real stumble into a gag before the house notices.

### Clinical psychologist
1. You are paid for hurting yourself on someone else's beat, and doing it well does you the most damage. A Perfect earns Laughs and paint, and more paint warps your inner world and makes your double stronger. The laughs you earn are spent just to stay upright inside, and standing still drains you. The only exits are walking away or performing for ever.
2. 3 of 5. The rules hold the right cost: success feeds the Stare and the Performer, and the walk back slumps lower the more he earned, which is how masking exhaustion really works. But in the moment every Perfect feels like a gain, and the bill comes later as a number. The stranger confirms it: "nothing I did made me feel hunted."
3. The game names the inner state instead of letting the player live it. The corridor thought ("I always feel I have lost a part of myself") and the quote cards spell out what masking clients mostly cannot put into words. Then the Mirror World turns that cost into enemies you can beat for loot. In the clinic, the cost of masking is not a monster. It is a quiet, spread-out loss: tiredness, not knowing what you actually want, and the mask doing its job so well that nobody notices anything is wrong. An arena with health, shards and a shop makes the inner world a place where you can win. The ending makes it worse: "I quit" plus a warm light of his own and applause is a single button to recovery, and the applause rewards him for unmasking in exactly the way the mask was chasing.
4. Each layer of Mask should add a little input lag to the clown's own stage timing next night, the same delay his reflection shows, so success makes it physically harder to act from himself.

### Core gamer
1. You perform on someone else's beat, and doing it perfectly earns a currency you must spend to survive the aftermath. Doing well makes you weaker (Mask makes the Performer stronger), and standing still gets you gnawed. Both ending choices (quit, or one more act with no judgement) leave you in the same loop. So: the harder you try to be liked, the more it costs, and there is no safe place to stop.
2. 2/5. The fall act has a real rhythm tell, and the "crash never lies" betrayal on night 3 is a good idea. But the stranger never felt hunted, and I would not either. A mis-timed fall costs almost nothing (two misses just end the act), the shop lets you buy everything, and the Mirror World's goal is unreadable. The pressure is told in text; the rules do not impose it.
3. The rules do not mean anything yet. The system punishes success (Perfect gives Mask, Mask makes the Performer tougher), but a player cannot see that trade-off when they choose. A good player's reaction is to go for Perfects anyway, because Perfect pays 10 Laughs, and nothing in play warns that it is a trap. If the cost cannot be felt in the moment, the "life is against me" reading is something the player is told, not something they learn by playing. The skill moment is also hard to read (60 px clown, 30 px stick, crowd heads covering the ring). Laughs are both health and ammo, but if they can jump from 89 to 289 and Shards read 0 until the shop, that tension is gone. The one tight loop here, tell then input then grade, is one gag repeated three times. Nothing escalates inside a night, and the Mirror World has no visible win or loss. In my genre, a bleak system works only when I can see it tighten and still choose to push.
4. Make the Mask cost visible at the moment of a Perfect (the face visibly thickens and the next fight's threat is shown), so the player chooses between a clean fall and a safe miss.

### Jam judge
1. You perform pain on cue to earn a currency. That currency is also your health, so you spend it fighting what the performing creates. Getting better at it (Perfects, bought acts) paints more Mask and arms the double, and the Light can be put out for a night but never beaten. Life, in this game, is a debt you service by hurting yourself, and the night ends in a ledger of costs, not a win. The ending offers a quit button or one more act with no judgement.
2. 3/5. The design is built so that skill, progress and standing still are all taxed, which is a strong structural argument that life is against you. But the one cold read says nothing felt hunted on night 1. The hostility lives in the text, and in the mirror half the player can't tell what is happening to him.
3. The game's thesis is hostility, but the build's only felt pressure is the Light and the draining screen edge, and those sit in the half the stranger could not read. The stage half is a polite rhythm minigame done three times. The crew is warm, the audience is mostly backs of heads, and a failed fall costs almost nothing visible. The cruelty is told, not done to you. The act of "falling on purpose" is sad, but a player who nails the timing feels competent and pleased. The game never makes him feel hunted, because the timing is fair by design and has a visible tell. A jam judge gets ten minutes. If the Mirror World's goal and result are unclear in that time, the theme is read from quote cards, not felt. The end-of-night summary also contradicted the play (the lead says this is a capture artefact, but a player will judge what he sees). Fun is thin: one gag, repeated.
4. Make the mirror half state its goal and its result in one line each (win, lose, what changed), and let the crowd's silence after a Perfect visibly cost him something, so success itself feels like a trap.

### Auguste clown
1. you earn your place by being hurt on someone else's beat, and doing it well costs you more. A perfect fall pays Laughs and more paint, the Laughs are spent as health and ammunition in the mirror, and the crowd never says thank you. Nobody is cruel. The need to be loved is the trap, and you can't stop standing in the light.
2. 3/5. The mirror half pushes hard: standing still drains you, the Light can't be beaten, and the Performer learns what you buy. The stage half doesn't push at all. Nobody did anything to me. I fell, I got paid, I was fine. The stranger says nothing made him feel hunted, and I believe him.
3. The fall is too clean. I've fallen about forty thousand times, and a fall is never the problem. The problem is the second after it, when the laugh comes and you can't tell whether they laughed at the trick or at you. Here the player presses a button on a drumroll, which is a rhythm game. The player also controls the fall and the bow, and has three tries with two misses allowed. A real fall that went wrong leaves a bruise tomorrow. Here a miss costs nothing but a caption. Nothing about the crowd feels like an enemy. They are backs of heads, silent, and they don't turn on you. The whiteface who calls "Now!" too early is the one true thing in the build, because the partner is the one who sets you up to fail. Right now the game is out to get the clown only in the mirror, where it's a fight, and the stage, where it should hurt, reads as a puzzle.
4. Let the crowd (or the whiteface) make the player miss on purpose, on night 1, so he feels set up by the people he is trying to please.

### Mobile producer
1. You perform on cue to someone else's beat. The better you do, the more of yourself you pay (Mask), and the reward is a currency you spend just to stay upright. Standing still is punished, you can never win outright (the Light is never beaten), and every purchase arms your own double. Life here is a treadmill that charges you for skill.
2. 3. The rules are built to corner you (drain when idle, an unbeatable Light, a shop that arms the enemy), but the stranger never felt hunted. The Mirror World goal was unreadable, health jumped unexplained in the capture, and night 1 punishes nothing visible. The hunt is in the design and in the text, not yet in the hands.
3. The session shape is weak for a phone. A night is a long chain of non-interactive steps: title, quote card, board, corridor shots, one act with only three presses, a silent walk back, the mirror, a world with no stated goal, the shop, the end card. The player's real input is about three taps of one timing gag, then a fight they cannot read. On a 6-inch screen, a 60 px clown and a 30 px yellow stick as the key tell are not readable, and the hint text in the hardest part is the faintest. Nothing tells the player on night 1 what a good night is, there is no retry and no visible win or loss, and end-card numbers contradict what they saw. A phone player gives you about 90 seconds before deciding. Right now the most memorable moments (the child's "...on purpose.", the cracked glass) are things they read, not things they do. The reason to return tomorrow is curiosity about the double, which is a thin hook unless night 2 shows its payoff within the first minute. Also, one long unskippable night with no natural pause point is hard to resume after an interruption on a phone.
4. Make the fall act and the first Mirror World fight readable and decisive on a phone, with a large tell, one stated goal line, and a clear win or loss result, so the first night ends with a verdict the player felt.

### Streamer

### Nietzsche
1. To live is to fall on another man's drumbeat, and every outcome is taxed: the Perfect fall thickens the mask and summons the Stare, the miss summons Shame, standing still is gnawed. Applause is a wage spent the same night merely to remain upright, and whatever you acquire arms your double against you. Life here is a debt that skill only increases.
2. 3. The rules are hostile at every door, which is correct; but the hand does not feel it on night 1. The stranger passed with one Perfect in three, bought everything, and reported "nothing I did made me feel hunted." The hostility is in the ledger, not yet in the wrist.
3. This is a game of bad conscience that knows only one verdict: guilty. Excellence is punished, failure is punished, rest is punished, so the player learns not that life is against him but that the author is, and he stops striving, since striving changes nothing but the name of the creditor. That is nihilism without its overcoming, pity dressed in greasepaint. Worse, the end offers two buttons with words on them. "I quit" is the last man's wisdom, and "one more act, no judgement" is only asserted. A Yes to recurrence that costs one click is no Yes. Nowhere does the play let a fall be wanted for its own sake.
4. On the last night, make the unjudged act a real fall the player must time himself, with no grade, no Laughs, no Mask and no end unless he chooses it, so that continuing is an act of the hand, not a sentence read off a button.

### Camus
1. A man falls on another's beat, is paid in laughter, and must spend that laughter the same night merely to remain standing; nothing carries over, and each skilful fall thickens the paint and arms his double. Life here is a wage that buys only the next shift, and excellence is the debt. The night ends at a mirror, the game at a choice between leaving and going on.
2. 3. The rules are hostile (Perfect feeds the Stare, stillness drains, the Light returns 15% wider), but the stranger passed with one Perfect in three and bought both acts without pain, so on night 1 the hostility is declared, not suffered.
3. You have built Sisyphus and then offered him a door. "I quit" walks him out of the ring into a warm light of his own, with applause: that is escape, and you reward it with the game's only tenderness. The other button, to go on knowing, is "the fight goes on", shown without judgement, which in practice means shown without anything. So the play teaches that lucidity leads to the exit, and that staying is the mistake of those who have not understood. This is consolation dressed as courage. And it is unearned in the hands: the player has not yet pushed the rock long enough to know its weight, because the nights forgive him and the mirror fight tells him neither its aim nor its result.
4. Give the warm light and the audience to the man who performs once more with his eyes open, and let "I quit" end in plain silence.

## Red team

Every item at 10 of 13 or more. Red team seat: Core gamer (proposed none of them), arguing the no case in full:

Group A: V1 (ending is a prize), C2 (put the Performer out, do not shatter it), C1 (paint stays), C3 (dark lamp stays), V5 (card pleads for "I quit"). One argument covers them: the panel is fixing a cutscene, and the cutscene is where the player has stopped playing. facts.md says the ending is "the warm light... audience... two worded buttons". The player has no input until the buttons. C1, C2 and C3 change what a viewer sees for roughly a minute after the last fight. A player who is hunted for three nights will read a boss that dies as a boss that died, whatever follows. Removing the shatter (C2) also removes the only clear success signal in the whole run. The stranger could not tell if he had won or lost the Mirror World at all (finding 7), and a fight that ends in an ambiguous "put out" repeats that failure on the one fight that matters. Players need to know they won in order to feel the hollowness afterward. Nihilism works through a won fight that means nothing, not through a fight with no result. Also, C3 stacks a second dark figure in the ring (Chaplin's own objection) at the moment the player must read two buttons, and C1 plus V5 stack three themes onto one beat. I do not think V1 itself has an honest NO case: 13 of 13 is right that the order of light before choice hands over a prize. My NO is on C2's method, not its aim.

Group B: V2 (Mirror World clearable and pays). Nietzsche and Camus are right, but for a different reason than the panel's. A fight you cannot clear is not nihilism, it is a bad game: facts.md says gnawing is "until he acts", and acting is the only verb the player has. The design's own rule, "Once every insecurity is down nothing gnaws", is what makes each room finishable. If nothing ever ends, the Mirror World becomes a timer, and the player cannot tell punishment from a bug (see stranger findings 6, 7, "I never saw a win or a loss"). The honest fix is to keep the clear but make the payout not matter (shards that cannot buy anything lasting), which is not what V2 says. So V2 REAL on the pay, weak on the clear.

Group C: V3 and C4 (fall act pays skill; the stage should hurt). The strongest NO: the fall act is the one place where the player is treated fairly, and that is why the night 3 lie ("the crash never lies") lands. A tell that lies only means something if the player first learned to trust it. Making nights 1-2 hostile (C4) spends the betrayal early, and a miss that carries a cost into the Mirror World doubles a punishment the player cannot see coming, which is the "unfair" kind my genre hates. The Psychologist and Nietzsche are right that cost belongs on success (paint on Perfect), not on failure.

Group D: NO LOCK. I voted LOCK and I still hold the honest NO case's best point: a lock on Quit that the player can only pass by beating the Performer is a difficulty gate, and players treat gates as challenges, not as cruelty. The 10 of 13 who object are right that it reads as freedom as reward.

## Build facts given to the seats (/tmp/panel/facts.md, verbatim)

### The Clown and the Mirror: what the game is and does (build 36ceaf9, nights 1-3 prototype, 24 Nov 2026)

A 3D browser game (phone and desktop) made for a game jam whose theme is "the game is out to get you". Nights 1-3 are playable as a box-and-shape prototype; art is partly final (the clowns and circus crew are finished 3D models; props and some figures are boxes).

#### One night, in order (what is built)
1. Title: a closed curtain. He sits at a dressing-table mirror; the player's first input finishes painting his clown face. A short quote card (a line from Osamu Dazai's "No Longer Human") shows before each show. He then puffs himself up in a show of bravado; his reflection copies it exactly.
2. The changing room: an act board on the wall. He picks one act from paper notes (his own act "The deliberate fall", plus one act lent or tried out tonight; acts he bought later).
3. The walk out: fixed-camera corridor shots. Circus crew, one action each: a rigger lays a cable ("Fall over it, they love that") and he pratfalls over it; the props girl gives him a pie; the strongman hands him a horn to honk; a stagehand's child watches him trip on purpose and quietly says "...on purpose." Then his own thought shows over the picture: "I do it to feel I belong. But when the act ends, whether it went well or not, I always feel I have lost a part of myself." Crew lines are warm on nights 1-2 and only polite on night 3.
4. The act in the centre ring (camera low behind the crowd; the crowd is backs of heads; the child watches from the entrance and never laughs).
   - The deliberate fall (new today): he walks into a spotlight pool; standing in it starts a drumroll; the band leader's stick rises before the crash; the player presses so that his trip lands on the crash. Graded Perfect / Good / "Nobody saw it. He fell in the dark." / "Too early" / "Too late. The crash hit a clown still standing." Three falls, two misses end the act. A Perfect pays 10 Laughs and one more layer of face paint (Mask); a Good pays 5. On night 3 the whiteface calls "Now!" too early; the crash never lies.
   - Other acts (spotlight sweep, talking scale, soldier drill, tit-for-tat) are timing games on an overlay screen, then the routine plays in the ring. In those acts the crowd pays extra for what hurt him (a fall, the pie in his own face, a trip the front rows saw).
   - After every act: the laughter stops, he stands alone in the light with a sad face; then he smiles and bows with his hat back on, dented. Caption: the crowd's reaction, Laughs, Mask.
5. The walk back: same corridor, empty. The more Laughs the act earned, the lower he slumps. The room is gloomier; flakes fall.
6. The mirror: he presses his hand to the glass; the reflection is a beat late; the glass cracks. A Nietzsche quote card (before going in or after coming out).
7. The Mirror World: a route through unworldly spaces (a mirror corridor, a photograph room, a pencil-drawn void) to an arena. Figures there are his insecurities, spawned by what he did in the act: each fall in the act feeds the Whisper; a Perfect brings the Stare; a miss brings Shame; the soldier drill brings the Sergeant; the talking scale the Weigher; the Light is always there.
   - Laughs are both his health and his ammunition: each gag (slap, pie, a dodge-fall) costs Laughs.
   - Standing still is never safe: the screen edge darkens at once and after a warning his Laughs drain ("they gnaw") until he acts.
   - The Light can only be put out for the night (three gags on its lamp), never beaten; the dark lamp keeps following him; it returns next night 15% wider.
   - Beaten figures drop shards. Losing wakes him at the mirror with half his shards.
8. The shop at the mirror: he buys acts with shards. Every act bought is learned by the Performer, his own pale double. Wiping an act off refunds half and leaves a stain.
9. End card: "The shame of living:" and a list of the night's costs; "Go on to night N".

Meters: Laughs (earned on stage, spent in the Mirror World, do not carry between nights). Mask: paint layers, earned by Perfects; more Mask makes the Mirror World warp more and makes the Performer stronger.

#### The last night (night 3, built as box and shape)
He has lost every act he bought: only slap, pie and the dodge-fall are left. He fights the night's insecurities, then the Performer, his double, who attacks with every act he bought (each attack has a tell); more Mask = more Performer health. When the last one is beaten he is bathed in a warm light of his own (not the monster Light); the audience appears one by one clapping, heard, then cut to silence. Two worded buttons: (1) he bows, says "I quit" and walks out of the ring; (2) he performs one last act, shown with no judgement: the fight goes on.

#### In the design but not yet built
- The reflection's lag grows each night.
- On the last night the menu's Quit is greyed out until the Performer is beaten.
- Crew tutorial lines turn into game prompts ("one more show!") on later nights; the shop always has one more act to sell.
